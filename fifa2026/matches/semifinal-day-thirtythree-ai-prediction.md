### Historical Backdrop
Five World Cup meetings, none forgotten: England 3–1 in the 1962 group stage; the 1966 quarter-final England won 1–0 while Argentina's captain Rattín was ordered off; **1986 in Mexico City — Maradona's Hand of God and the Goal of the Century in the same 2–1 afternoon**; 1998 in Saint-Étienne — Owen's wonder-goal, Beckham's red, 2–2 and **Argentina through on penalties**; and 2002, when Beckham's penalty won the group-stage rematch 1–0. England lead the World Cup ledger three wins to one (plus the shootout), but Argentina own its two most mythologised moments — and this is the first meeting at any World Cup since 2002, and the first world semi-final between them ever. One more wrinkle: **Messi, in a 21-year international career, has never played England**.

### Simulation Report — **England 0–0 Argentina (a.e.t., England win 3–1 on penalties)**
For 120 minutes the machine played the tightest match of its tournament. Possession split down the middle — **51% Argentina, 49% England** — and the midfield turned into a demolition derby: **193 tackle contests combined** (England winning 55 of their 98, Argentina 52 of 95), the highest-contact match the simulation has produced. England were the more dangerous side in every period — **22 shots to 13**, with the pattern holding 8–4, 9–6 and 5–3 across the two halves and extra time — but the evening belonged to the men in gloves. **Bukayo Saka fired thirteen shots**, more than any player on either side, and none of them went in; **Julián Álvarez** answered with eleven of Argentina's thirteen and met the same wall. Kane managed five, Bellingham four; **Messi, his sprints rationed on a third straight extra-time cycle, just two** — the free-role economist waiting for a moment that never opened. The high England line held Álvarez's channel runs; Paredes' screen kept Bellingham from the pockets that decided Miami. Ninety minutes: 0–0. Thirty more: still 0–0. Penalties — with **Emiliano Martínez**, the most feared shootout keeper of his generation, standing where England's history says the story ends.

Instead the machine wrote the inversion. **Kane — first up — missed. Bellingham missed.** But between and after them Argentina kept failing at the same job: Paredes, Mac Allister and Enzo Fernández all went without scoring, and suddenly the kick that mattered most belonged to **Saka — the man whose missed penalty decided Euro 2020 — and he buried it.** Rice converted. Álvarez briefly kept Argentina alive, and **Elliot Anderson**, the least-heralded starter on the pitch, struck the kick that ended it: **3–1**, and England into a World Cup final for the first time since 1966 — against the same opponent that final's mythology keeps arguing with.

**AI takeaway:** The machine sees the fixture's entire history repeating with the polarity flipped. In 1998 England out-played Argentina for long stretches and lost from twelve yards; here they out-shot them in every period, hit the frame of the match thirteen times through one winger, and needed the shootout anyway — then won it while their two biggest names missed, because the takers behind them refused the script. That Saka's redemption kick came against **Emi Martínez**, the keeper built for these nights, is the kind of detail no screenwriter would risk. Argentina's tournament of late escapes — Enzo at 90+ against Egypt, Messi at 97 against Switzerland's ghost of 2014, Álvarez at 112 in Kansas City — finally met a night with no late door to walk through. The machine's final is set: **Spain vs England, July 19, MetLife Stadium** — the Euro 2024 final, replayed for the world title.
