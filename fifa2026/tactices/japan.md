# Japan — Tactical Profile

## Identity & Philosophy
Hajime Moriyasu's Japan is Asia's gold standard: a positional, technical, intensely-pressing side that has dismantled Germany and Spain in recent World Cup memory and dominated AFC qualifying with the goal difference of a European top side. The identity is "European football played at Japanese tempo" — every pass has a purpose, every press is choreographed, and every transition is a five-pass move rather than a hopeful long ball. Moriyasu's group has matured into a confident outfit that no longer parks the bus against elite opposition; they go toe-to-toe. The squad has absorbed two pre-tournament blows — captain and midfield destroyer Wataru Endō withdrew with a foot injury and retired from international duty (Ko Itakura inherits the armband), and first-choice winger Kaoru Mitoma is out with a hamstring — yet the side has only grown into the tournament. Japan opened Group F by twice coming from behind to draw the Netherlands 2-2, then dismantled Tunisia 4-0 in Monterrey behind a brace from Ayase Ueda. Four points from two games leave them level with the Netherlands at the top, second only on goal difference. The mentality is unbroken: dominate the ball, run opponents into the ground, and bury chances early and late.

## Formation
- Shape: **3-4-2-1** — Moriyasu's signature back-three system. Three centre-backs, two flying wingbacks, a double pivot, two free "shadow strikers" behind a lone centre-forward. In settled possession it morphs into a **3-2-5** (the wingbacks push high, the pivot splits); out of possession it drops into a compact **5-4-1** mid-block. Against the Netherlands Moriyasu flexed this into a back-four block at moments, but the base identity remains the back three.
- Role mapping (roster order in `japan.yaml`):
  - index 0: GK — **Zion Suzuki** — modern sweeper-keeper, comfortable starting the build-up with feet, sprints out to clear long balls behind the high line.
  - index 1: LCB — **Hiroki Itō** — left-footed ball-playing centre-back on the left of the back three, steps into midfield to break lines and covers behind the left wingback.
  - index 2: CCB — **Ko Itakura** — the new captain and central anchor of the back three, the calmest passer on the team, organises the line and steps up to break lines with vertical passes.
  - index 3: RCB — **Takehiro Tomiyasu** — Japan's most complete defender, right of the back three; aggressive stepper out of possession, can play every position across the back.
  - index 4: LWB — **Keito Nakamura** — left wingback, the engine on the flank: gives width going forward, whips in early crosses, sprints back to make a back five out of possession. Scored Japan's first equaliser against the Netherlands.
  - index 5: DM — **Kaishu Sano** — the new ball-winning screen in front of the back three, stepping into the role vacated by Endō. High-energy destroyer with elite stamina (17); tackles, recovers, recycles, and almost never crosses the halfway line.
  - index 6: CM — **Daichi Kamada** — the progressive, line-breaking half of the pivot and Japan's chief central creator. Takes the ball off the back three, turns into space, threads through-balls, and arrives late in the box — his deflected header completed the comeback against the Netherlands.
  - index 7: RWB — **Junya Itō** — right wingback, pure vertical pace (speed 17); hugs the touchline, beats his man on the outside and delivers early low crosses. Sprints back to make a back five out of possession.
  - index 8: LAM — **Takefusa Kubo** — left-sided shadow striker, free role; drifts inside off the left into the right half-space, slips passes through, the technical genius of the side.
  - index 9: CF — **Ayase Ueda** — the lone 9, presses from the front, holds the ball up, finishes inside the six-yard box. A classic centre-forward and Eredivisie top scorer, not a false 9.
  - index 10: RAM — **Ritsu Doan** — right-sided shadow striker with a winger's instincts; drifts between the lines, tucks inside onto his left foot to shoot, the team's leading goalscorer in qualifying. Late runs into the box.

## Style of Play

### Build-up
Patient short build-up out of a **back three**: Itakura sits central while Hiroki Itō and Tomiyasu split wide, and Sano-Kamada form the double pivot in front, giving a **3-2** base. The wingbacks (Nakamura left, Junya Itō right) push high to pin the opposition fullbacks, turning the shape into a **3-2-5**. Suzuki is the eleventh outfielder — always offering a back-pass option. Japan is utterly comfortable playing through the first line of pressure: 60-70% possession against most opponents. Sano shields, Kamada is the line-breaker, and the CBs (especially Itakura and Itō) are licensed to carry into midfield. The first switch of play usually goes to Kubo finding space in the left half-space.

### Pressing
**Intense, coordinated, high block** — Japan presses from the front whenever the opposition GK touches the ball. Ueda curves his run to lock the GK onto one side; Kubo and Doan cut the passing lanes to the centre-backs; the wingbacks Nakamura and Junya Itō jump the opposition fullbacks; Sano and Kamada step up to win the second ball. The press trigger is a back-pass to the GK or a CB taking a heavy first touch. When the press breaks, Japan does NOT chase — they retreat into a compact **5-4-1** mid-block and start again. Stamina is a national strength — every outfielder is rated 15+ in stamina.

### Defensive shape
Compact **5-4-1**: the wingbacks Nakamura and Junya Itō drop to form a back five, Kubo and Doan tuck into a midfield four alongside Sano/Kamada, Ueda leads the line alone. The back three holds a medium-high line — Itakura and Tomiyasu step up aggressively to compress space while Itō covers. Tomiyasu rarely gets dribbled past 1v1. Japan concedes few clear chances because the block is narrow (15 units between the back line and the midfield four) and the second balls are won by Sano.

### Wide play
Asymmetric. **Left side:** Kubo drifts inside off the half-space, leaving Nakamura to provide the width and the overlap as the natural touchline runner. **Right side:** Junya Itō stays wide and attacks the outside with raw pace, while Doan tucks inside off him onto his left foot to shoot; the right of the back three (Tomiyasu) covers the channel behind. The chief creative axis is Kubo–Kamada–Doan–Itō; final-ball patterns flow right-to-left-to-right with one-touch combinations.

### Final third
Combination football inside the box: Kubo's cut-back to Doan arriving late, Doan's left-foot curler from the right, Ueda's near-post header from a Nakamura or Junya Itō cross, Kamada arriving late from deep. Japan does NOT shoot from distance; everything is engineered for a clean angle inside 18 yards. When transitions arrive, Doan and Kubo sprint diagonally toward the opposite post; Itō provides the outlet run down the right.

## Set Pieces
- Attacking corners: **Doan** in-swingers from the right, **Kubo** out-swingers from the left. Primary targets: Tomiyasu (near post), Itakura (penalty spot), Ueda (back post).
- Defending corners: hybrid — four zonal markers across the six-yard line, three man-markers (Sano tracks the most dangerous runner), two short-corner blockers.
- Free kicks: Kubo direct from the right half-space (left foot), Kamada direct from central range, Junya Itō from the right.
- Penalties: **Ueda** primary, **Doan** secondary, **Junya Itō** tertiary.

## Key Player Notes
- **Kamada (6):** The new creative hub of the midfield. With Endō gone, Kamada's progressive passing and late box arrivals (he scored the 89th-minute equaliser vs the Netherlands) carry Japan's central threat. Pairs with Sano's destruction to form a balanced pivot.
- **Sano (5):** The screen in front of the back three, inheriting Endō's positional discipline. He does not move past midfield; his ball-winning and stamina (17) are the foundation of Japan's defensive solidity.
- **Itakura (2):** The new captain and the calmest passer in the back three. Organises the line, steps out to break pressure, and is a primary aerial target at attacking set pieces.
- **Kubo (8):** The free role. Drifts off the left into the right half-space. Almost zero defensive tracking expected — he is conserved for transitions and final-ball moments. Japan's technical talisman with Mitoma absent.
- **Tomiyasu (3):** The most tactically intelligent defender. Anchors the right of the back three; can play every position across the back line if needed.
- **Doan (10):** A leading scorer, deployed as the right-sided shadow striker. Cuts inside, shoots early. His finishing (15) is elite for an Asian wide player.
- **Junya Itō (7):** The right wingback and the fastest man in the squad (speed 17). Stretches the pitch on his own; his early low crosses are Ueda's primary supply line.
- **Nakamura (4):** Versatile flank weapon who scored Japan's first goal against the Netherlands; gives the left its width and an early-cross supply.
- **Suzuki (0):** The modern sweeper-keeper. His comfort with the ball at his feet unlocks Japan's back-three build-up.

## Tournament Mindset
Japan arrives at the World Cup believing they can reach the quarter-finals or further — and the squad has done it on paper. The mentality is no longer "park the bus and counter against Germany"; it is "out-press Germany." Drawn in Group F alongside the Netherlands, Tunisia, and Sweden, Japan opened with a gritty 2-2 comeback against the Dutch, then put Tunisia away 4-0, and now face Sweden in the final group game at AT&T Stadium in Arlington — effectively a knockout for top spot. A win guarantees qualification and, depending on the Netherlands–Tunisia result, can lift Japan to first in the group. Sweden are wounded — beaten 5-1 by the Netherlands after thrashing Tunisia 5-1 themselves — but dangerous on the counter and physically imposing, so Japan must respect the transition while pressing them onto the back foot. Even shorn of Endō and Mitoma, expect Japan to dominate possession and play boldly. Stamina is their hidden superpower — they will run opponents into the 80th minute and find a goal in the 87th, as the Netherlands learned. Discipline ratings are uniformly 14+ — they will not get red-carded.
